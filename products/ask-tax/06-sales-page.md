## 2. The turn

> ### Here is what the research actually says
>
> You have been told, in some form, that women do not ask.
>
> It is not true, and it stopped being true a long time ago. Across large samples, 64% of women reported trying to negotiate for a promotion or better pay, against 59% of men. On job offers it is 54% of women against 44% of men. Women ask more.
>
> The pay gap did not move.
>
> So the problem was never the asking. Something is happening after the ask, and it has been studied carefully enough that we can name it.
>
> When a woman makes an assertive request, she is rated more competent and less likeable. The likeability score is the one that decides promotions. Make the same request as a man and you are a good negotiator. Make it as a woman and you are demanding, or difficult, or the word people use when they think you cannot hear them.
>
> That is a fee. It gets charged every time you ask, it never appears on a payslip, and nobody told you the rate.
>
> I call it the ask tax. Once you can see it, you can stop paying it.

## 3. Why the usual advice fails

> You have probably tried some of this.
>
> **"Be more confident."** You are not short on confidence. You are managing a jobsite. The problem is not the volume of the ask, it is what happens in the room after it.
>
> **"Know your worth."** You do know. You have known for two years. Knowing it has not moved your band.
>
> **"Just ask."** You asked. Twice. You got told there was no budget, and then you watched a req open in March.
>
> **"Be assertive."** This is the one that does real damage, because it is the exact behaviour the research shows gets penalised. Being told to be assertive, and then being punished for assertiveness, is not a personal failure. It is a contradiction you were handed and told to solve alone.

## 4. The second problem nobody sells you a fix for

> ### You can run a perfect conversation and still lose
>
> Here is the part that takes people out.
>
> Your manager agrees. Your numbers are good. The conversation goes well.
>
> Then the actual decision happens in a calibration room you are not invited to, among people who have never heard your case said out loud by anyone.
>
> The data on this is unusually clear. Mentorship shows no measurable correlation with whether women get promoted. Sponsorship does, at close to twice the rate. And 31% of entry-level women have a sponsor, against 45% of men.
>
> You are probably over-mentored and under-sponsored. Three people willing to give you coffee and advice. Nobody saying your number in the room where it counts.
>
> Every course in this category teaches you the conversation. None of them teach you the room.

## 5. What this is

> ### The Ask Tax
>
> A system for making the ask without paying the fee, and for getting your case into the room you are not in.
>
> It is not a course. There are no modules and no six-week journey. You have a comp cycle in nine weeks and a job that already takes fifty hours. This is built to be used the night before a meeting.

### The mechanism

> Six steps, in a fixed order. Each one maps to a specific, documented way these conversations fail.
>
> **1. The third-party anchor.** The number enters the room attached to published band data, not to you wanting it. This removes the "she is asking for herself" read that triggers the penalty in the first place.
>
> **2. The scope ledger.** One page of everything you have absorbed since your last comp change, written in your employer's units instead of yours. It turns a conversation about how hard you work into an accounting conversation. They do not pay for effort. They pay for scope, and scope only counts when it is written down.
>
> **3. The warmth frame.** A fixed sentence pattern that attaches your ask to a shared outcome before the number lands. This is the piece that keeps your likeability score intact while the competence claim gets made. Both, in order, or neither works.
>
> **4. The silence hold.** You say the number. Then you stop talking. Scripted, timed and rehearsed, because this is where most people lose the money they already won. The pause feels unbearable and it lasts four seconds.
>
> **5. The written follow.** A same-day email that locks what was agreed, in your manager's own words. A verbal yes with nothing in writing is not an agreement. It is a polite way to end an uncomfortable conversation, and it is where most wins quietly die between March and December.
>
> **6. The proxy ask.** You recruit one person with standing to say your number in the room you are not in, and you hand them a paragraph short enough that they will actually use it. This is the step that converts a won conversation into a won promotion.

## 10. Objections

> **"Is this going to make me look aggressive?"**
> The opposite is the entire design. Steps 1 and 3 exist specifically to keep the competence claim from landing as a personality problem. That is what the warmth anchor is for, and it is why the order is fixed.
>
> **"My company has strict bands. There is no room."**
> Bands have tops. Most people sit well below the top of the one they are already in, and do not know where it ends. The band data pack tells you. Half the people who run the audit find their money inside their current band, with no promotion required.
>
> **"I already tried negotiating and it went badly."**
> Then you have better information than someone who has not. The backlash playbook was written for exactly that, and step 6 exists because a bad first conversation is usually a room problem rather than a script problem.
>
> **"Can I expense this?"**
> Frequently yes, under professional development. There is a template email for that request in the pack, which is itself a small, low-stakes rehearsal of everything else in here.
>
> **"What if I am not in construction or engineering?"**
> The mechanism is not industry-specific but the band data and vocabulary are built for construction, engineering, industrial and enterprise tech. If you are outside those, some of it will not fit and I would rather say so now.

## 12. Implementation notes

**Do not add testimonials yet.** There are no customers. Fabricated or composite social proof in a category whose buyers verify things is a refund event and a reputation event. Ship without it. Replace this block after the first 25 buyers, using the day-90 outcome survey in file 05. Real numbers from real people will outperform anything invented, and this ICP can smell the difference.

**Claims compliance.** Every dollar figure on this page is framed as arithmetic or as a research finding, never as a typical result. Keep it that way. "Most people who run the audit find between $18,000 and $40,000" describes audit output, not earnings. If you later add outcome data, it needs a disclosure and it needs to be true. Meta enforces on this and refund disputes turn on it.

**Verification blocker.** The 64%/59% and 54%/44% figures, the sponsorship numbers and the 7-in-10 and 3-in-4 statistics all come from the bank in file 01, which carries a fidelity caveat in section 1.7. Complete the 90-minute link-verification pass before this page goes live. Citing a study incorrectly to this audience is worse than citing nothing.
